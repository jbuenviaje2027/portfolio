    Serendipi-Tea

    Designing an innovation hub experience to foster authentic connection among startup founders in accelerator spaces.

    Client
    StartX, Stanford
    Role
    Head User Researcher
    Timeline
    Apr – Jun 2025
    Team
    Team Jello (3 designers)

    Research at a glance

    17people reached
    6needfinding interviews
    4founder segments
    3prototypes tested
    35–45min playtest sessions
    Needfinding interviewsSegmentationPOV synthesisRapid prototypingConcept testingObservational playtests
    The problem

    At StartX, nearly every conversation between founders feels like a pitch. Real, lasting connection gets pushed to the back burner.

    What I did

    As Head User Researcher on a team of three, I led needfinding (17 people reached, 6 interviews), and we prototyped three ideas before choosing one.

    What we built

    A cozy third space at StartX with a connection wall, snacks, and a card game called We're All Humans.

    What happened

    Playtimes ran 35 to 45 minutes. Founders talked about love, death, politics, and insecurities, and laughed, reflected, and surprised each other.

    Founders don't necessarily need more networking spaces. They need spaces that are explicitly not that.

    Project overview

    A card-based conversation tool built through a human-centered design process, prototyped and tested with founders across the StartX accelerator community.

    Built with Team Jello (a team of 3) as part of Design 121 at Stanford's d.school, in partnership with StartX. I led the project as Head User Researcher.

    The challenge

    How might we use the human-centered design process to design an innovation hub for the next generation of startup founders at Stanford Research Park?

    1

    Needfinding

    17People reached
    6Interviews conducted
    4Founder segments
    Key takeaway: lots of diversity, in tenure, background, and what “connection” even means to a founder.
    2

    Synthesis

    Segment A

    Seasoned founders

    Keeps up with the latest tech trends and is always looking for ways to boost productivity, typically moving between spaces to work, talk, and present his newest slide deck.

    Segment B

    Brand new founders

    Wants more genuine talks with other founders beyond concise business chat and tool-sharing, but feels an invisible gap that keeps those conversations from happening.

    Segment C

    Underrepresented founders

    Feels like funding is her largest barrier to entry, largely due to how she's perceived as a minority founder, but remains resilient and channels that feeling to keep pushing.

    User group: StartX

    Brand new founders

    How might we promote serendipitous conversations between founders?

    New StartX founders who lack an environment conducive to organic, serendipitous connection with one another, making the average conversation feel transactional.

    JB
    POV statement

    New members of the StartX community crave an environment free of artificial, network-based connection, to ensure the relationships they form through StartX programming have a lasting impact.

    3

    Prototyping

    Problem framing

    In environments where every conversation feels like a pitch, genuine and authentic connection always feels like it's placed on the back burner.

    VS

    StartX founders feel naturally authentic with each other, seamlessly connecting with other members of the community as if they were family.

    In short: we want founders to create deeper, lasting connections.

    Three prototypes

    Enter→Look around→Snacks→We're All Humans card game→Photo→Exit
    Prototype 01

    Founder Quilt (X)

    Elevates underrepresented voices by displaying local founders.

    Prototype 02

    We're All Humans

    Humanizes founders by creating space for personal discussion.

    Prototype 03

    Third Space

    Creates a cozy environment inside a typically busy startup ecosystem.

    Insights

    Third spaces deepen connection

    People were willing to interact with our prototype, typically in pre-existing groups.

    We need a wide variety of topics

    When the focus is on getting personal, people get deep.

    Smaller and cozier means intimacy

    Conversations feel deeper with smaller groups of people (2 to 3).

    Food is a way to invite people

    Food transitions to being less important than an intrinsic desire to connect.

    Creating the cards

    Our cards were heavily inspired by the game We're Not Really Strangers, ranging from light reflection (Seed level) to deeper conversation (Bloom level), plus questions written directly from the experiences of startup founders.

    Cards were designed in Figma, printed on cardstock, corner-cut for a rounded feel, then sprayed with lacquer for a refined finish. We also built a custom box with a “How to Play” card.

    View the card deck on Figma

    Seed“Who was your biggest role model growing up?”
    Sprout“Who was your childhood best friend?”
    Bloom“What's something you're grieving, even if it's not obvious loss?”

    Creating the experience

    01 / 04

    Cards were placed at a campus cafe space with signage, and left to let the experience run on its own. A digital camera was placed within the set-up so users could document their own experience.

    From insight to design

    Each design choice traces back to something we observed in testing.

    Food is a way to invite peopleFood drew people in, then faded behind the desire to connect.Snacks and drinks as an early step in the experience flow, before the card game.
    A wide variety of topics; personal focus gets people deepWhen the prompts got personal, conversations did too.Three card levels, from light reflection (Seed) to deeper conversation (Bloom).

    Final solution at StartX

    Connection wallFood and drinksWe're All Humans card game

    Design result

    01 / 02

    Variety of strong emotionLaughter, reflection, surprise

    One of the more surprising outcomes was how quickly the founders' guards came down. Most of them spend their days pitching, networking, and performing a version of themselves built for a room full of investors or competitors. But once there was a vessel built strictly for connection, with no ask, no agenda, and nothing to prove, that professional mask seemed to just fall away. Conversations that started as polite small talk kept sliding into something more honest: grief, doubt, the parts of running a company nobody really talks about. It made the case that founders don't necessarily need more networking spaces. They need spaces that are explicitly not that.

    6

    Takeaways

    Rooting my work in user needs

    My group and I were unfamiliar with the experience of startup founders. This project stemmed directly from the conversations we had with them, and the pain points we noticed.

    Designing for emotional impact

    This project taught me that meaningful design goes beyond solving logistical problems. It can create space for vulnerability, connection, and authentic human interaction.

    Conversation as a designed experience

    This project reframed dialogue itself as something that can be intentionally crafted through prompts, pacing, environment, and material interaction.

    If I ran this again

    Looking back, here is how I would make the research more measurable.

    Quantitative

    Define success metrics first

    Set them before testing: session length, cards played per session, and a 1 to 5 “I felt connected” rating after each game.

    Experiment design

    Add a control

    Run a no-cards version of the same space to see whether the game, and not just the room, drove deeper conversation.

    Qualitative rigor

    Code conversations with a rubric

    Tag topics on a simple depth scale (surface, personal, vulnerable) and have a teammate code a subset to check agreement.

    Longitudinal

    Follow up a week later

    The goal was lasting connection, so I would check whether founders kept talking after the session.

    Project overview

    During an 8-week internship partnered with Stanford's Global Studies Program, I joined Simone Acc. Collection, a leather goods manufacturer based in Ui-Wang, South Korea, working across both their in-house design team (0914) and their Global Sales team (Tory Burch).

    Goal 2

    Goal 3

    Strengthen design visualization

    Communicate ideas clearly through concept sketching and design pitch decks, moving efficiently from concept to sample.

    Goal 4

    Experience Korea

    Make the most of my first time working and traveling abroad.

    Understanding Simone

    What is Simone?

    Simone Acc. Collection is a leading Korean luxury handbag and accessories manufacturer, known for producing high-quality leather goods for global designer brands.

    What is 0914?

    0914 is Simone's own premium leather-goods brand, built to showcase the company's craftsmanship, material quality, and design expertise. The brand focuses on timeless, human-centered, small-batch design.

    Understanding trends

    01 / 12

    Common threads: ornamentalism, functionality, pockets, and playful looks balanced with earth tones.

    User group: 0914

    0914 customers

    How might we meld current fashion trends with 0914's brand?

    0914 customers value artisan-level craftsmanship and high-quality materials. They struggle to find leather goods on the market that feel uniquely human, rather than designed for the mass market.

    This board reflects a direction rooted in tactility, slow craftsmanship, and objects that carry a sense of history.

    What it is

    Node Foundation is a gallery dedicated to digital and on-chain art. I'm a Guest Experience Associate there, from June 2026 to now.

    What I do

    I guide guests through new exhibitions, explain the art, and keep the gallery running: setup, guest assistance, and front desk coverage.

    Project overview

    Node Foundation is a gallery dedicated to digital and on-chain art. As a Guest Experience Associate, I help visitors, many encountering this kind of art for the first time, actually understand and connect with what they're looking at.

    1

    Role at a glance

    From observation to practice

    Small observations from the floor that changed how I host.

    What I observedHow I responded
    Many guests arrive without context for digital or on-chain art.Explain it clearly without over-explaining, meeting people where they are.
    Layout shaped comfort: sightlines, lighting, and where I stood.Treated guest experience as spatial design as much as hosting.

    4

    Takeaways

    Translating new art forms

    A lot of guests walk in without much context for digital or on-chain art. Learning how to explain it clearly, without over-explaining, pulled directly from the same instincts I use as a Peer Advisor and TA: meeting someone where they are.

    Seeing the full exhibition lifecycle

    Being on the install side and the guest-facing side of the same exhibitions gave me a much better sense of everything that happens between an artist's concept and a visitor's first reaction to it.

    Designing for learning and connection

    Guest experience turned out to be spatial design as much as it was hosting. How a room is laid out (sightlines, lighting, where I stood relative to a piece) shaped whether someone felt comfortable getting curious or just walked past.

    Making it enjoyable, not just educational

    Digital and on-chain art can feel intimidating on paper. Part of the job was translating that seriousness into something people could actually enjoy: pacing conversations and keeping explanations light so a visit felt digestible, not like a lecture.

    If I ran this again

    Looking back, here is how I would make the research more measurable.

    Quantitative

    Add a two-question exit survey

    Ask “How comfortable did you feel with the art, 1 to 5?” and “What do you wish you had known?” to track change over time.

    Qualitative

    Log recurring questions

    Tally the questions guests ask most, turn the top ones into a short wall guide, and see whether the same questions fade.

    Observation

    Use a simple protocol

    Record dwell time at each work and where guests hesitate, to test how layout and my positioning affect engagement.

    Comparison

    Compare guided and unguided visits

    A small side-by-side would show whether a short welcome changes how long and how deeply people engage.

    Weaver of community

    As an RA, a Design Program peer advisor, and a lover of people, I attempt to create communities and spaces where people feel comfortable and seen.

    Woven, not accidental

    Community isn't something that just happens. It gets woven, one small invitation at a time.

    I attempt to create communities and spaces where people feel comfortable and seen. Here's how that's played out in the dorms and at the d.school.

    100+freshmen paired by shared interests
    2freshmen I got to mentor in the sibling program I piloted
    1new d.school tradition, Newly d.Clared
    1

    In the dorms

    Resident Assistant, Florence Moore Hall and Toussaint Louverture House

    As an RA, I spearheaded multiple dorm programming events to bring people together. Two of my favorites:

    Frosh Plates

    I paired over 100 freshmen based on shared interests to spark meaningful connections within our community.

    A sibling mentorship program

    I piloted a sibling mentorship program, and I got to be a mentor myself to two incredible freshmen!

    2

    At the d.school

    Peer Advisor and Community Lead, Stanford d.school

    I created a new declaration event called Newly d.Clared to celebrate the milestone of declaring the design major. The d.school now uses it as a tradition.

    I also help with class exhibitions and design programming, so students' work has a place to be seen and gathered around.

    Every community has moments worth marking. I like building the room for them.
    3

    How I weave

    Start from what people already love

    Frosh Plates worked because it began with interests. People connect faster when there is already something to talk about.

    Pair people across experience

    A sibling program gives new students someone a step ahead, and gives mentors the chance to grow too.

    Celebrate the milestones

    Declaring a major is a big moment. Newly d.Clared makes sure nobody passes through it alone.

    Make the work visible, then gather around it

    Exhibitions turn a quarter of private effort into something a whole community can stand around and talk about.

    Why design around community?

    If we want people to feel seen, we have to design for it on purpose.

    Almost everything we design lands in a group of people: a dorm, a classroom, an accelerator, a gallery. Nobody uses those spaces alone. They use them with and through each other.

    Left to chance, spaces quietly teach us how to behave. At StartX, I watched a room teach founders to pitch instead of connect. In the dorms and at the d.school, I've seen the opposite: one small, intentional invitation can teach people that they belong.

    That's why I design for community first. The best products, programs, and places aren't just usable. They make room for people to meet each other.
